AIPW and double robustness

Published

November 18, 2026

Lecture brief: View the source brief.md · How these notes are made

Lecture outline
  • Construct an estimator by correcting outcome predictions with observed residuals.
  • Define double robustness and see what it buys when one working model is wrong.
  • Prove the property by expressing the remaining bias as a product of errors.
  • Explain how cross-fitting supports learned adjustments, and why confidence intervals require more than consistency.

AIPW: predict, then correct

Consider a randomized tutoring experiment. Previous test scores help predict end-of-term scores, so adjusting for them may improve precision. But averaging predicted treatment effects would trust the prediction model completely. Instead, we can use the experiment to estimate what the predictions missed.

There are \(N\) students with fixed pretreatment covariates \(X_i\) and potential outcomes \(Y_i(1),Y_i(0)\). Assignment is \(Z_i\in\{0,1\}\), and, assuming no interference and consistency, \(Y_i^{\mathrm{obs}}=Y_i(Z_i)\). The target is

\[ \tau=\overline Y(1)-\overline Y(0), \qquad \overline Y(z)=\frac1N\sum_iY_i(z). \]

The design supplies known probabilities \(\pi_i(z)=\mathbb P_Z(Z_i=z)>0\) for both arms. Let \(a_z(X_i)\) predict student \(i\)’s outcome under arm \(z\). Initially, these predictions are fixed before assignment; they need not be correct.

For either arm, decompose the target into an average prediction and an average prediction error:

\[ \overline Y(z)=\frac1N\sum_i a_z(X_i) +\frac1N\sum_i\{Y_i(z)-a_z(X_i)\}. \]

The first average is available for all students. In the second, we observe the error only when \(Z_i=z\). Estimate that average error by HT weighting:

\[ \widehat\mu_{z,\mathrm{AIPW}} =\frac1N\sum_i\left[ a_z(X_i)+\frac{\mathbf1\{Z_i=z\}}{\pi_i(z)} \{Y_i^{\mathrm{obs}}-a_z(X_i)\}\right]. \tag{1}\]

This is augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW). Subtracting the control estimate from the treated estimate gives \(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\widehat\mu_{1,\mathrm{AIPW}} -\widehat\mu_{0,\mathrm{AIPW}}\). The construction is the arm-specific version of the prediction-plus-residual correction behind GREG in L02 (Cassel, Särndal, and Wretman 1976; Tsiatis et al. 2008).

The justification is short. For fixed predictions,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_Z\left[ \frac{\mathbf1\{Z_i=z\}}{\pi_i(z)} \{Y_i^{\mathrm{obs}}-a_z(X_i)\}\right] &=Y_i(z)-a_z(X_i),\\ \mathbb E_Z(\widehat\mu_{z,\mathrm{AIPW}}) &=\overline Y(z). \end{aligned} \]

Inverse weighting recovers the average residual, including any systematic prediction error. Hence the treatment-effect estimator is design-unbiased. This argument uses marginal assignment probabilities and works for complete as well as Bernoulli randomization.

Why predict at all? HT weights the full outcomes; AIPW weights the residuals. Good predictions can remove much of the variation that random treatment imbalance would otherwise turn into estimation noise. Bad predictions can increase variance, even though the fixed-prediction estimator remains unbiased. Fitting predictions on the experiment’s own outcomes requires additional care, which we address below.

What does “doubly robust” mean?

The experiment gives us the correct assignment probabilities. What if these probabilities must instead be estimated? AIPW has a useful protection: consistency can survive an incorrect propensity model or an incorrect outcome model.

To state this precisely, switch to an i.i.d. sample \(O_i=(X_i,Z_i,Y_i^{\mathrm{obs}})\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), from a population law \(P\). Our target is now the population ATE, \(\tau_P=\mathbb E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}\), rather than the average over a fixed set of experimental units. Define

\[ e(x)=\mathbb P(Z=1\mid X=x), \qquad m_z(x)=\mathbb E\{Y(z)\mid X=x\}. \]

Assume consistency, no interference, conditional exchangeability \(\{Y(1),Y(0)\}\perp Z\mid X\), and overlap \(\epsilon\le e(X)\le1-\epsilon\) for some \(\epsilon>0\). These assumptions identify \(m_z(x)\) as \(\mathbb E(Y^{\mathrm{obs}}\mid Z=z,X=x)\) and imply \(\tau_P=\mathbb E\{m_1(X)-m_0(X)\}\).

Definition 1 (Double robustness) An estimator based on two nuisance-model components is doubly robust if it is consistent when either component is correctly specified and consistently estimated, even if the other is misspecified, subject to the common identification and regularity conditions.

For AIPW estimation of \(\tau_P\), the two components are:

  1. the propensity score \(e(x)\);
  2. the pair of outcome means \(m_0(x),m_1(x)\).

Thus \(\widehat\tau\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\tau_P\) if the propensity fit is consistent, or if both outcome-mean fits are consistent. The other fitted component must still be well-behaved; for example, inverse weights cannot have zero denominators.

“Nuisance” means that these functions support estimation of \(\tau_P\) but are not themselves the target. The definition is a statement about consistency under a union of two models, not a claim of exact finite-sample unbiasedness (Bang and Robins 2005). Also, one correct arm-specific outcome regression alone does not generally suffice.

To examine the property, let \(q(x)\) be a working propensity bounded away from zero and one, and let \(a=(a_0,a_1)\) be working outcome means. For now these are fixed functions; later they can represent limits of fitted models. Define an observation’s adjusted contrast as

\[ \begin{aligned} \phi(O;a,q)={}&a_1(X)-a_0(X)\\ &+\frac{Z}{q(X)}\{Y^{\mathrm{obs}}-a_1(X)\}\\ &-\frac{1-Z}{1-q(X)}\{Y^{\mathrm{obs}}-a_0(X)\}. \end{aligned} \tag{2}\]

AIPW averages these contrasts. Outcome regression alone averages \(a_1(X)-a_0(X)\); unaugmented IPW averages \(ZY^{\mathrm{obs}}/q(X)-(1-Z)Y^{\mathrm{obs}}/\{1-q(X)\}\). The next example compares their population limits.

What double robustness buys: one model can be wrong

Suppose tutoring participation is observational. There are two equally common baseline groups, \(X=0\) and \(X=1\), with the following true quantities:

Group Propensity \(e(x)\) Treated mean \(m_1(x)\) Control mean \(m_0(x)\)
\(0\) \(1/4\) \(2\) \(1\)
\(1\) \(3/4\) \(4\) \(1\)

Assume the identification conditions just stated. The population effect is \(\tau_P=\{(2-1)+(4-1)\}/2=2\).

Consider two possible modeling errors. A propensity fit might ignore the difference in uptake and converge to \(q(x)=1/2\) in both groups. An outcome fit might underpredict treated outcomes, converging to \(a_1(0)=1\), \(a_1(1)=2\), while retaining \(a_0(x)=1\). The latter misspecifies the outcome component even though its control regression is correct.

The table reports probability limits, assuming the fits converge to the indicated functions and the required averaging conditions hold.

Correct components Regression only IPW only AIPW
Both \(2\) \(2\) \(2\)
Outcome means only \(2\) \(2.5\) \(2\)
Propensity only \(0.5\) \(2\) \(2\)
Neither \(0.5\) \(2.5\) \(2.25\)

Here is how AIPW repairs the third row. The incorrect outcome predictions give an average contrast of \((0+1)/2=0.5\). With the correct propensity, the expected treated residual correction is

\[ \frac12\left[ \frac{1/4}{1/4}(2-1) +\frac{3/4}{3/4}(4-2)\right]=1.5. \]

The expected control residual correction is zero. Adding the correction recovers \(0.5+1.5=2\). Regression alone misses the target; AIPW does not.

In the second row, the outcome means are correct. Residuals then have conditional mean zero in each arm, so even the incorrect weights leave their expected correction at zero. AIPW retains the correct regression answer. IPW alone gives \(2.5\) because its constant weights do not correct the groups’ unequal treatment probabilities.

This is the practical benefit of double robustness: the same estimator remains consistent in either failure scenario, without requiring the analyst to know which model is correct. It does not detect which model failed. When both are wrong, the treated correction is \(1.75\), giving \(0.5+1.75=2.25\). There is no general guarantee of consistency, lower bias, or lower variance in that case.

Establishing double robustness

The example suggests two mechanisms. Correct weights recover a nonzero average residual; correct outcome means make the residual’s conditional mean zero. We can prove both by one calculation.

Condition on \(X=x\). For the treated contribution,

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E\left[ a_1(X)+\frac{Z}{q(X)}\{Y^{\mathrm{obs}}-a_1(X)\} \,\middle|\,X=x\right]\\ &\quad=a_1(x)+\frac{e(x)}{q(x)}\{m_1(x)-a_1(x)\}\\ &\quad=m_1(x)+\frac{q(x)-e(x)}{q(x)}\{a_1(x)-m_1(x)\}. \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

For control, replacing \(e,q\) with \(1-e,1-q\) gives a remainder \(\{e(x)-q(x)\}\{a_0(x)-m_0(x)\}/\{1-q(x)\}\). Subtract the control contribution and average over \(X\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\{\phi(O;a,q)\}-\tau_P =\mathbb E\bigg[(q(X)-e(X)) \bigg\{&\frac{a_1(X)-m_1(X)}{q(X)}\\ &+\frac{a_0(X)-m_0(X)}{1-q(X)}\bigg\}\bigg]. \end{aligned} \tag{4}\]

The error is a product of propensity and outcome errors. It is zero if \(q=e\), or if \(a_0=m_0\) and \(a_1=m_1\). With convergent fitted functions, controlled denominators and moments, and a justified law of large numbers, this identity establishes the consistency property in Definition 1. Combining two fitted models without such an identity would not establish double robustness.

The identification assumptions remain common to both routes. Correct observed-data regressions do not remove unmeasured confounding, and augmentation cannot recover outcomes from a treatment arm that is never observed in part of the target population.

A brief connection to trimming

For a fixed retained set \(A\) with positive probability, define \(\tau_A=\mathbb E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X\in A\}\). Repeat the calculation conditional on \(X\in A\): the same cancellation holds for that target, requiring correctness on the retained set.

Khan and Ugander (2025), published online in 2024, use augmented estimation and product-rate conditions for inference after data-adaptive trimming. Their target averages conditional effects over the actually retained sample covariates. Learning that set requires additional regularity; the fixed-set argument alone does not justify adaptive-set confidence intervals. Nor does double robustness mean that either a correct propensity model or a correct variance model suffices to select the oracle trimming set.

Learning the adjustment: many controls and cross-fitting

In a randomized experiment, the propensity component is known. The remaining opportunity is to learn useful outcome predictions, perhaps from hundreds of baseline variables. We need accurate predictions, not accurate inference on every regression coefficient. Regularization can help when unrestricted regression is unstable. But fitting predictions on the experiment’s own outcomes makes those predictions depend on its assignments.

An extreme example exposes the danger. Suppose students have distinct covariate vectors, and an arm-specific learner memorizes outcomes for students in that arm and predicts zero for everyone else. All observed residuals vanish. The resulting AIPW contrast is just

\[ \frac1N\sum_i\{Z_iY_i(1)-(1-Z_i)Y_i(0)\}. \]

With Bernoulli assignment probability \(1/2\), its design expectation is \(\tau/2\). Perfect training fit has eliminated the residual correction rather than made it accurate.

Cross-fitting separates training from evaluation. Partition observations into \(K\) folds \(\mathcal I_1,\ldots,\mathcal I_K\) independently of the records. In a fixed-population experiment, fix the partition independently of assignment. For each fold:

  1. Fit the outcome regressions on the other folds. In an observational study, fit the propensity there too.
  2. Predict the held-out observations and evaluate their AIPW contrasts.
  3. Average all held-out contrasts, giving each observation equal weight.

If \(k(i)\) is observation \(i\)’s fold and \((-k)\) denotes training without fold \(k\), the i.i.d. estimator is

\[ \widehat\tau_{\mathrm{CF}} =\frac1n\sum_i \phi\bigl(O_i;\widehat m^{(-k(i))},\widehat e^{(-k(i))}\bigr), \tag{5}\]

where \(\widehat m^{(-k)}\) contains the two fitted outcome functions. In a randomized trial, use the known assignment probabilities instead of fitting \(\widehat e\).

Under independent Bernoulli assignment without interference, conditioning on a fold’s training data fixes its predictions without changing its evaluation assignments. The earlier design-unbiasedness proof then applies fold by fold, provided fits are well-defined and integrable. Complete randomization, cluster assignment, and interference require different dependence arguments; splitting rows alone is not sufficient.

For practical fitting, choose the folds in advance. Select features and tune penalties using only each training sample, for example through inner cross-validation. Prespecify a fallback if a training sample lacks one treatment arm. Do not choose the model or fold split because it gives the most favorable treatment estimate.

Wager, Du, Taylor, and Tibshirani (2016) develop high-dimensional regression adjustment and cross-estimation for randomized experiments. A central lesson is that prediction-risk consistency—convergence of out-of-sample squared prediction error to that of the true conditional mean—can support efficient treatment-effect estimation under appropriate conditions. Recovering the exact nonzero regression coefficients is not the goal. Their particular exact and asymptotic guarantees depend on the estimator and assumptions, not just on the name “cross-fitting.”

Double robustness, precision, and double machine learning

Double robustness protects consistency when one nuisance component is wrong. The product identity also gives a second benefit when both components are learned accurately: the remaining bias can be smaller than either nuisance error.

Let \(\|f\|_2=\{\mathbb E[f(X)^2]\}^{1/2}\) and suppose \(c\le q(X)\le1-c\) for some \(c>0\). By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \left|\mathbb E\{\phi(O;a,q)\}-\tau_P\right| \le \frac1c\|q-e\|_2 \left(\|a_1-m_1\|_2+\|a_0-m_0\|_2\right). \tag{6}\]

Conditional on training, substitute the fitted functions into this bound. If, for example, each prediction error is \(o_p(n^{-1/4})\), the product is \(o_p(n^{-1/2})\), smaller than the usual sampling fluctuation of an average. Equal rates are not necessary; it is the product that matters. If the propensity is known exactly, its error factor is zero.

The absence of a first-order bias term at the true nuisance functions is Neyman orthogonality. Double/debiased machine learning combines orthogonal estimating equations with flexible nuisance learning and cross-fitting (Chernozhukov et al. 2018). AIPW for the ATE is one instance. Orthogonality controls sensitivity to small nuisance errors; cross-fitting controls dependence between fitted functions and evaluation observations.

These ideas do not make “doubly robust” synonymous with “valid confidence interval.” One consistently fitted component can suffice for consistency, but root-\(n\) inference needs additional rate and regularity conditions. An incorrect nuisance limit can change which estimation errors enter at first order. The advanced section states a sufficient set of conditions for the usual AIPW standard error.

For experimental work, the main payoff is straightforward: known assignment probabilities supply one correct component, while learned outcome predictions can improve precision. For observational work, the extra correct-model route is useful, but both routes still depend on identification.

Advanced material: a standard error for cross-fitted AIPW

Remain in the i.i.d. population-ATE setting. Let \(\eta_0=(m_0,m_1,e)\) denote the true nuisance functions, and write \(\phi(O;\eta_0)=\phi(O;m,e)\). The centered true contrast is

\[ \begin{aligned} \psi(O)={}&m_1(X)-m_0(X)-\tau_P\\ &+\frac{Z}{e(X)}\{Y^{\mathrm{obs}}-m_1(X)\}\\ &-\frac{1-Z}{1-e(X)}\{Y^{\mathrm{obs}}-m_0(X)\}. \end{aligned} \tag{7}\]

It has mean zero. In the identified ATE model, this is the efficient influence function (Hahn 1998).

One convenient sufficient regime has a fixed number of folds of comparable size, bounded outcomes and outcome predictions, propensities and their fits bounded away from zero and one, and, for every fold,

\[ \begin{gathered} \|\widehat e-e\|_2=o_p(1),\qquad \|\widehat m_z-m_z\|_2=o_p(1),\quad z=0,1,\\ \|\widehat e-e\|_2 \left(\|\widehat m_1-m_1\|_2+\|\widehat m_0-m_0\|_2\right) =o_p(n^{-1/2}). \end{gathered} \]

Fold superscripts are suppressed here. Boundedness can be weakened with suitable moment conditions. Under these conditions,

\[ \widehat\tau_{\mathrm{CF}}-\tau_P =\frac1n\sum_i\psi(O_i)+o_p(n^{-1/2}). \tag{8}\]

There are two remainders behind this result. The product bound controls the conditional mean error of the fitted contrast. Conditional on a fold’s training data, the additional centered evaluation error has variance at most its squared prediction error divided by the fold size. That error vanishes at the needed scale when the fitted contrast converges in squared mean. This argument works fold by fold; it does not assume that the overlapping training sets are independent.

Let \(\widehat\phi_i\) be observation \(i\)’s held-out contrast from Equation 5. If \(V=\mathbb E\{\psi(O)^2\}>0\), a consistent variance estimate and an asymptotic 95 percent interval are

\[ \widehat V=\frac1n\sum_i (\widehat\phi_i-\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{CF}})^2, \qquad \widehat\tau_{\mathrm{CF}}\ \pm\ 1.96\sqrt{\widehat V/n}. \tag{9}\]

This is an i.i.d. population-ATE interval, not an exact finite-population or network-design interval.

To interpret precision, define \(d(x)=m_1(x)-m_0(x)\) and \(\sigma_z^2(x)=\operatorname{Var}\{Y(z)\mid X=x\}\). Conditional residual means are zero, so the law of total variance gives

\[ V=\operatorname{Var}\{d(X)\} +\mathbb E\left\{ \frac{\sigma_1^2(X)}{e(X)} +\frac{\sigma_0^2(X)}{1-e(X)} \right\}. \tag{10}\]

The residual variances show the value of predicting outcomes; the denominators show why limited overlap can remain costly even with ideal predictions. In a randomized trial with known propensity, cross-fitting and prediction consistency suffice for this oracle comparison under the other stated conditions; no \(n^{-1/4}\) outcome-prediction rate is needed.

Further reading

  • Robins, Rotnitzky, and Zhao (1994) develop foundational augmented-weighting ideas in missing-data problems; Bang and Robins (2005) give a primary treatment of double robustness.
  • Tsiatis et al. (2008) connect augmentation to precision in randomized trials; Wager et al. (2016) develop high-dimensional adjustment and cross-estimation.
  • Chernozhukov et al. (2018) give the broader orthogonal-score and cross-fitting framework, including the conditions behind its inferential guarantees.
  • Khan and Ugander (2025) extend augmented estimation to adaptive trimming. Their target definition is essential to interpreting the result.
